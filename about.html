01 Where it started

The original solved a real problem. When you work with sites across Canada, “what time is it there?” comes up constantly. It grouped places that share a clock time into one card and showed each card's offset from Toronto, all computed live from IANA time zones.

What it couldn't do:

  • show where anything is, or which sites are in daylight
  • let anyone other than me add places, or use anywhere but Toronto as the reference
  • answer the follow-up question: when can we all meet?

A bug I hadn't noticed showed up in the first screenshot review: the clock face radius (78) was larger than its 88-pixel SVG canvas, so every analog clock rendered as a clipped square.

03 What v2 does

Your board, on a planet

Every place is a pin on a WebGL Earth. The cards still cluster places that share a clock time, and each card's colour matches its pins and arcs.

Real day and night

The terminator is computed from the sun's position each frame. NASA's Blue Marble and Black Marble imagery blend across it, so city lights switch on as dusk passes.

Meeting planner

Shows each card's working hours on your timeline and highlights when everyone overlaps. Click a column and the whole globe jumps to that moment.

Scrub time

Drag ±24 h or run at up to 1000×, forwards or backwards. Clocks, sunlight and satellites all move together.

Share by link

A group is serialized into the URL, so sending a link shares your board with no accounts and no server.

Live orbits

Over 10,000 real satellites, an orbit ring and predicted passes for any one you pick, and a sky plot of what is above your site right now.

04 Architecture

Plain TypeScript, no UI framework. One SimClock is the single source of truth for time: every component reads clock.now(), so a single scrub moves everything consistently.

05 Engineering highlights

10,000 satellites at 60 fps

Running SGP4 propagation for every satellite on every frame would drop frames. Instead a Web Worker returns position and velocity snapshots a few times a second, and the vertex shader moves each point with a second-order step under two-body gravity, p + v·dt + ½·a·dt². Motion stays smooth at 1000× with a worker that is idle most of the time.

DST-correct by construction

There are no hand-written offset tables. Offsets, abbreviations and wall-clock conversions all go through Intl.DateTimeFormat and IANA zone names. The tests prove the v1 card logic regroups correctly in winter: Whitehorse joins Calgary, and Regina joins Winnipeg, because Saskatchewan and Yukon don't change their clocks.

A data pipeline that's polite

The cloud sandbox Claude Code ran in couldn't reach CelesTrak, the public orbital-data source, so we changed the design. A scheduled GitHub Action fetches a fresh snapshot twice a day and ships it with the site. Visitors load satellites from the same origin, CelesTrak gets 2 requests a day instead of one per visitor, and the app still falls back to a live fetch if the snapshot is stale.

Physically placed sunlight

The sun's sub-solar point comes from the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision formula, accurate to about 0.01°, and is tested against the 2024 solstice and equinox. The Earth shader blends the day and night maps across a soft terminator, adds an ocean glint from a water mask, and a fresnel atmosphere.

Labels that don't collide

Pins carry HTML labels that follow them in 3D. A greedy pass places them each frame in priority order (hovered, your site, focused, card leads) and drops any that would overlap. Labels near the right edge flip to the left, and labels behind the Earth are hidden by a ray–sphere occlusion test.

Zero-backend sharing

Groups are UTF-8 encoded and base64url'd into the URL fragment, so they never reach a server log. On the way back in, every field is validated: coordinates are bounds-checked, time zones are verified, and lengths are capped.

06 Bugs caught in review

Claude Code drove a headless Chromium browser, screenshotted every mode, and we reviewed the images together. Typecheck and unit tests passed throughout; these issues only showed up visually:

  • Overlapping labels. Regina was hidden under Winnipeg. → priority-based collision pass.
  • Black halo in Cream theme. The atmosphere shader wrote alpha = 1, which normal blending paints solid. → output the glow as alpha.
  • Flat orbit ring. The camera flew to directly above the satellite, which sits in its own orbital plane, so the ring was seen edge-on. → tilt along the orbit normal.
  • Panel wouldn't scroll. A flex child without min-height: 0. → one line of CSS.
  • Giant pins when zoomed in. Markers were sized in world units. → scale with camera distance.
  • Muddy twilight band. A tint along the terminator read as a stripe. → removed; the blend alone looks better.

07 How I worked with Claude Code

My job was the product decisions: scope, audience, which parts of v1 had to survive, and what “good” looked like. Claude Code's job was the implementation, plus the questions I hadn't thought to ask. It planned the architecture, wrote the TypeScript and shaders, wrote and ran the tests, set up CI, and checked its own work in a real browser.

What made it work:

  • Ask before building. Two rounds of multiple-choice questions turned a vague brief into a spec in minutes.
  • Bring prior work. Sharing v1 gave it a design language to keep, instead of a blank page.
  • Make it prove things. Tests for the logic, screenshots for the visuals. Most of the real bugs above were caught by looking.
  • Treat blockers as design input. The network restriction turned into a better data architecture.
