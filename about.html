Case study · Deven Chandel · October 2026

A time board that does one job well.

A controller in a National Operations Centre covers sites in every Canadian time zone. When something is scheduled, they need three answers fast: what time is that at each site, who is on shift, and how do I write it in the report? I built this with Claude Code. The first big version I shipped got the looks right and the job wrong. This page is about that, and about the rebuild.

01 v1: the original board

I wrote the first version to look up time zones for report timestamps. Its one good idea was to put places that share a clock time in the same card and show each card's Δ against a reference site. That idea survives in every later version.

What it couldn't do:

  • convert a future event to every site
  • show who is on shift
  • work for anyone whose sites or reference weren't hard-coded

The first screenshot review also caught a v1 bug: the clock face radius (78) was larger than its 88-pixel canvas, so every analog clock was clipped into a square.

02 v2: the detour

My first brief was “something that looks good on LinkedIn”. The result was Time Board 3D: a WebGL globe with a live day/night terminator, plus a bonus mode tracking 11,000 real satellites, with GPU motion and pass predictions. It worked and it looked great.

Then I looked at it as the person it was meant for. A controller doesn't need Starlink. They need to know whether Whitehorse is staffed at 02:00 Eastern. My verdict was “jack of all trades, master of none.” So I wrote a new brief: an NSOC tool, with no satellites, that serves its purpose. That meant deleting about 2,900 lines of working code.

03 v3: the brief and the decisions

Claude Code asked four multiple-choice questions before building anything. My answers became the spec:

  1. Main viewTimeline board + mapOne row per clock across 24 h. The globe becomes the secondary map.
  2. JobsAll fourEvent converter, events with countdowns, copy-for-reports, and shift / on-air hours.
  3. ReferenceNSOC, set onceAsked on first visit and remembered. Every Δ is “vs NSOC”.
  4. SharingBrowser + linkNo accounts, no backend. Share links and JSON backups.
  5. Kept from v1The design languageColours, fonts, analog clocks, offset grouping, Dark/Cream.
  6. CutSatellites, everywhereCode, dependency, CI data job, cron, docs.

04 What it does now

Read every site at once

Each row runs on its own local hour ticks, so you can see Newfoundland's half-hour shift and where midnight falls for each site. Daylight and staffed hours are drawn on the same track.

Convert any event

Type 1400, a date and a site. Every row jumps to that moment. If DST skips or repeats that time, or a site is unstaffed then, it says so.

Paste-ready reports

14:00 ET / 13:00 CT / … / 15:30 NT, ordered the way Canadian ops reports read: NSOC first, then west, then east.

Events and countdowns

Broadcasts, maintenance windows and shift changes. Each shows a live countdown, the local time everywhere, and how many sites are staffed at the start.

Shift coverage

Group-wide or per-site hours. Overnight shifts and 24 h coverage both work, and so do the 23 h and 25 h days when clocks change.

Anyone's sites

Make a group, add any of 2,360 cities or click the map, and share it by link. The recipient gets your sites, hours and events, measured from their NSOC.

05 Architecture

06 Engineering highlights

Times that don't exist, or happen twice

On the spring-forward night, 02:30 never happens. On the fall-back night, 01:30 happens twice. The converter detects both and says so, instead of quietly picking one. Events are stored as wall time plus zone, so a daily 07:00 handover stays at 07:00 local all year.

Shifts across midnight and DST

A 22:00–06:00 shift is 7 h on the spring-forward night and 9 h in autumn, and the tests check both. Every shift boundary is converted through the zone, never by adding 8 hours.

Labels a report can trust

Intl calls Whitehorse “GMT-7” and gives St. John's a long name. An override table pins MST and CST for the zones that don't change their clocks, so a report never says “MT” for two different times.

Colour that works for everyone

The eight group colours come from a validated palette. I ran a colour-vision-deficiency checker against both app themes: all checks pass in Dark, and Cream passes with visible labels. Status colours are kept for staffing only and always come with an icon and a word.

Nobody loses their setup

Saved data from the previous version is migrated: groups, theme and pinned site are kept, and dead satellite settings are dropped. Share links from v1 still open, and the test suite includes a real v1 link.

A map that can't break the tool

The 3D map loads on demand. If WebGL is off, only the map disappears, and the board, converter and events keep working. On phones it isn't loaded until you open the Map tab.

07 Caught before shipping

Tests passed throughout. These bugs were caught by Claude Code's code-review skill and by driving the real app in a headless browser:

  • Two maps at once. Switching tabs while the map was still loading started a second WebGL canvas. → the map now loads once, even if requested twice.
  • Handovers that skipped “now”. A zero-length daily event jumped to tomorrow the second it started. → a one-minute “now” window.
  • Feb 31 called a DST gap. An impossible date was treated as a spring-forward time. → dates are checked against the length of the month.
  • Shared hours dropped. Opening a link for a group you already had threw away its hours and events. → new events are merged in; a different version is imported as its own group.
  • Two Kingstons. The tooltip matched sites by name, so same-named sites mixed up their counts. → sites are matched by their clock.
  • Stale warnings, stale titles. A DST warning, or the previous event's title, stayed up after a new conversion. → every conversion resets both.
  • The window jumped. Converting a time re-centred the timeline and hid the now line. → the window stays anchored on now.
  • Hover lag (v2). Picking among 11,000 satellites took 30 ms per mouse move. → 0.56 ms, before satellites were cut.

08 How I worked with Claude Code

My job was product: who the tool is for, what to keep from v1, what to cut, and when something wasn't good enough. Claude Code did the engineering, and I asked it to use only the skills and agents the task needed:

  • A Plan agent read the codebase and wrote the redesign plan. It found four problems before any code changed, including that removing satellites would break the globe's time sync.
  • The dataviz skill set the timeline's rules: one crosshair for every row, fixed colourblind-safe colours, status colours only for staffing, and a table view behind every chart.
  • The run skill drove the real app in headless Chromium. It took screenshots of every mode and theme on desktop and phone, and ran 13 end-to-end checks covering conversion, clipboard, share link, reload and more.
  • The code-review skill reviewed the whole change and found ten issues, all fixed with regression tests.

One honest note: I also tried to bring about 300 third-party skills from my PC into the cloud session. Claude Code's safety check blocked installing untrusted instructions into its own configuration, and I agreed. That stays my decision to make on my own machine.
